# Chapter Techniques

Adapted from zenstory-ai's story-long-write and story-short-write method
cards (MIT): emotion-first planning, one technique per chapter, and the
hook rules. The style-sheet.md of this project wins; this card plans beats,
it never overrides voice.

## Emotion first, then structure

Every scene serves one emotion the reader should feel at the end of it —
dread, vindication, grief, wonder, relief. Name the emotion before
drafting; if a beat does not serve it, tension, or the chapter's purpose,
cut it. One core pivot per chapter: the chapter is about one turn, one
revelation, or one cost. Subplots pass through; they do not settle here.

## Pick one technique per chapter

Match the technique to the chapter's main function — at most one:

- **Reversal** (identity, knowledge, or standing flips): charge the
  misunderstanding early, plant the truth under the reader's nose, land the
  flip on the chapter's peak page. The reversal re-contextualizes what came
  before; it does not add new facts at the moment of the flip.
- **Pressure / face-down** (vindication, comeuppance): stack the
  humiliation or cost first; the release only pays after the charge. Never
  let the win be free.
- **Emotional pull** (relationship, loss, loyalty): the turn is a choice
  with a cost either way. Earn the feeling with a concrete memory or
  detail; do not name the emotion twice.
- **Suspense / unease** (mystery, dread): withhold one specific thing the
  reader wants; give two things they did not know they wanted. Escalate by
  specificity, not by adjective.
- **Quiet charge** (expectation, promise): a small concrete event sets an
  expectation the book will cash later. Plant it and move on.

None of the five fit? Write the chapter plain — a bridge chapter earns its
place by moving people, information, or cost.

## Hooks

- **Opening**: the first lines carry a specific person in a specific
  situation under pressure — not weather, not philosophy, not recap. Cold
  opens on action are allowed when the action is the situation.
- **Closing**: end on action, sharp dialogue, or an open complication
  (see deslop-gates gate 6). A question the reader now holds beats a
  trailer tease. New debts at the close are good; resolved summaries are
  not.
- Before drafting, reread the previous chapter's close: this chapter's
  open answers it, reframes it, or delays it on purpose. Never ignore it.

## Verify before you deliver

From the long-write contract list: check the word band, the events that
must happen, the events that must not, the timeline anchors, and the
chapter's stopping point. `story continuity .` catches the timeline and
state; `story pacing .` shows whether the hook and outcome actually landed.
