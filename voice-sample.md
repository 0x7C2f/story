## Demonstration

Elias Thorne rubbed his temples, trying to massage away the persistent ache that always accompanied a deep dive. The archive node hummed beneath his fingertips, a sleek obsidian curve amidst the clutter of his dimly lit workspace. Screens flickered with lines of raw sensory data, the digital lifeblood of millions of citizens in New Providence. As a memory archivist, his job was routine: catalog, compress, and store. But today, the routine was broken.

The file designated M-774-B was flagged as a standard childhood recollection—a trip to the coastal seawall, circa 2042. But when Elias initiated the playback, the sensory feedback spiked into the red. It wasn't the crash of waves or the smell of synthetic brine that flooded his neural link; it was static, sharp and violent, followed by a fleeting, crystal-clear image of a corporate holding facility.

He leaned closer to the monitor, his breath catching in his throat. Memories were supposed to be immutable, protected by layers of cryptographic seals. Altering one without fracturing the subject's psyche was considered theoretically impossible. Yet, here it was. The timeline metadata showed no signs of tampering, but the raw code underneath the visual overlay was bleeding into neighboring sequences. Someone had clumsily spliced a fabricated childhood over a highly classified, traumatic event.
